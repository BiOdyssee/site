The Brown Pelican

The brown pelican (Pelecanus occidentalis) is an iconic species found along the coasts of the Caribbean Sea 🌊.

Recognizable by its brown plumage and long, characteristic bill, it is one of the most fascinating seabirds of the region. This pelican is known for its spectacular dives to catch food, mainly fish 🐟.

Distribution 🌍 :

The species is present along the coasts of Guadeloupe, but it can also be found in other areas of the Caribbean Sea, as well as in Central and South America, from the southern United States to eastern Brazil.

Threats ⚠ :

Although their populations are increasing, brown pelicans face several threats. Water and beach pollution, especially plastic ♻, poses a direct danger to their food supply and habitat 🏝. In addition, human disturbance, particularly related to tourism development 🏖, disrupts their tranquility, which is essential for their reproduction.

Conservation Measures 🌱 :

Various initiatives have been implemented to protect this species, both locally and internationally 🌍. Preserving nesting sites and reducing marine pollution are priorities. Public awareness programs and measures to limit human disturbance in sensitive areas are being put in place to ensure a future for these magnificent seabirds. In Guadeloupe, the species benefits from a special protection status.

🦎 The Marbled Anole (Anolis marmoratus) — a small, colorful reptile!

The Marbled Anole is a common endemic species of the islands of the Guadeloupe archipelago 🏝️. Insectivorous and arboreal, it occupies a wide range of habitats, from humid or dry forests to mangroves, and even gardens and cultivated areas. It plays a key role in controlling invertebrate populations 🪲.

Morphologically, the species shows notable sexual dimorphism, with males often larger and possessing a colorful dewlap used for territorial communication and courtship. This small lizard stands out for its great chromatic variability, displaying different spots and marbling in shades of green, brown, and orange that vary according to location.

These morphological differences are widely studied by scientists. Indeed, the species is at an evolutionary crossroads, where the boundaries between ecotypes, subspecies, and distinct species are blurred. On the main island of Guadeloupe alone, there are currently 6 recognized subspecies of the Marbled Anole.

Individuals inhabiting the island of La Désirade perfectly illustrate this blurred distinction. With their colors and patterns adapted to a drier, more open environment, this population has recently been elevated from subspecies status to a distinct species: Anolis desiradei!

Despite its high adaptability, the Marbled Anole is on the front line of predation by introduced species (cats, rats, mongooses, etc.). The detection of the Cuban Anole in 2023 (Anolis sagrei), a species with which it competes, also represents a threat.

🟢 Meeting the Green-throated Carib in Guadeloupe (Eulampis holosericeus) ✨

Endemic to the Lesser Antilles, the Green-throated Carib is one of the jewels of Guadeloupe’s biodiversity. This small bird, often seen hovering around tropical flowers, plays an essential role in island ecosystems as a major pollinator.

🔍 Easily recognizable by its iridescent green and blue plumage, it has a long, slender bill perfectly adapted to reaching flower nectar. Its ability to hover in mid-air, made possible by extremely rapid wingbeats, allows it to feed while effectively pollinating many plant species.

🌿 It can be found in a wide variety of habitats: dry coastal forests, mesophilic forests, gardens, and even urban areas. It particularly favors tubular, nectar-rich flowers, but also feeds on small insects, supplementing its protein intake.

⚠️ Although relatively common in Guadeloupe, the species still faces several threats:

  • Habitat loss due to urbanization
  • Use of pesticides reducing food resources
  • Degradation of natural habitats and ecological corridors
  • Extreme climatic events intensified by global change (hurricanes, storms…)

🛡 Conservation measures & possible actions

  • Preserve and restore natural habitats
  • Promote wildlife-friendly gardens with local nectar-producing plants (hibiscus, heliconias, balisiers…)
  • Limit the use of pesticides
  • Raise awareness about the role of pollinators

✨ The Green-throated Carib reminds us that even the smallest players have a key role in the dynamics of tropical ecosystems.

🌵 Melocactus intortus intortus, a cactus with a funny head! 💚

This cactus is nicknamed the “Englishman’s Head” because of its resemblance to the traditional headgear worn by the British guard 💂. It is an endemic species of the Lesser Antilles, found only on Saint Martin, Saint Barthélemy, and in Guadeloupe on the islands of Terre-de-Haut and La Désirade 🏝️.

Its most remarkable feature is the cephalium that grows at the top of the plant. From this structure emerge flowers and later fruits once sexual maturity is reached. These inflorescences grow successively on top of one another, so that the oldest individuals end up wearing true top hats!

Unfortunately, the Englishman’s Head Cactus is severely threatened in its natural habitat. Its range is limited to a few islands with very dry climates, and grazing pressure as well as illegal collection strongly impact the remaining populations.

In Guadeloupe, Melocactus intortus intortus has been legally protected since 1988.

Nevertheless, conservation efforts are underway. On La Désirade, the joint work of the Jardin botanique du Désert and the La Désirade Nature Reserve ensures the reproduction and reintroduction of many individuals, helping to secure the future of the species. 🌵
